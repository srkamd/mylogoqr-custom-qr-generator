How to Turn Your Logo into a Scannable QR Code (Step-by-Step)

M Written by MyLogoQR Technical Team — October 29, 2026

Want to transform your company emblem or personal avatar into a scannable QR code? Here is the exact process to turn your logo into a high-performing QR code without technical glitches.

Key takeaways
  • You are not converting the logo into data — you are embedding it in the centre of a standard QR code.
  • A compact icon version of your logo works far better than a wide wordmark at small sizes.
  • Level Q or H error correction is what pays for the space the logo occupies.
  • One master SVG, generated once and tested, should serve every placement afterwards.

Step 1: Prepare Your Logo File

The quality of your central logo directly affects how professional the final barcode appears. Follow these graphic design tips before uploading:

  • Use Square Dimensions (1:1 Aspect Ratio): Center icons look best when cropped to a perfect square (e.g. 512x512 pixels).
  • Use a High-Contrast PNG with Transparent Background: A transparent PNG allows circular white badges to frame your logo naturally without awkward rectangular borders.
  • Simplify Complex Details: If your logo has small tagline text below it, remove the text and use only the core icon mark. Small micro-text inside a QR code becomes illegible when printed under 3 cm.

Step 2: Enter Your Destination Link or Contact Data

Choose what happens when someone scans your code. You can link to your homepage, an Instagram bio link, a direct WhatsApp chat greeting, or a digital business card.

Step 3: Upload and Scale the Logo Inside the Generator

Inside our generator tool, navigate to Section 5 (Logo) and click 'Upload logo'. Keep the Logo Size slider at 20% to 25%. This leaves over 75% of the data dots completely unobstructed, ensuring reliable scanning performance across both older iPhone and Android models.

Step 4: Set Error Correction to Level Q or H

Error correction compensates for the area covered by your logo. Selecting Level Q or Level H ensures that your smartphone camera easily reads the code even in low-light environments.

Real-World Sizing Rule of Thumb: Maintain a 10:1 scanning ratio. For every 10 inches (or centimeters) of distance between the customer and the QR code, increase the barcode width by 1 inch (or centimeter). A menu scanned from across a table (20 inches away) should be at least 2 x 2 inches wide.
Field-Tested Note: We always recommend running the 'Two-Phone Daylight Test' before sending any QR code to a commercial printer. Test scan the exported vector on both an iPhone and an older Android phone—first under direct bright sunlight, and then in a dimly lit hallway. If both open in under one second, your design is print-ready.

Turning a logo into a code vs putting a logo in a code

Two different ideas hide behind the same search. The first — the one this page is about — places your existing logo in the centre of a normal, reliable QR code. The second tries to reshape the code's modules until the pattern itself draws your logo. Artistic codes are impressive in a portfolio and risky on a coffee cup: the more the pattern is bent toward a picture, the less spare data remains to survive glare, damage and cheap cameras. For anything a customer must scan, choose the centred-logo method in the free QR code generator with logo: the brand is unmistakable, the code stays a code, and error correction H guards the middle where your mark sits.

Step-by-step: from logo file to finished code

Begin with the destination, not the design — type the link, contact or Wi-Fi details and confirm they open correctly, because a beautiful code to the wrong place is just expensive confetti. Upload the squarest, simplest version of your logo, set its size near a fifth of the code, and choose brand colours that keep the modules clearly darker than the background. Add a frame label if the code will stand alone in public ("Scan for the menu"). Then export twice — SVG for the printer, PNG for everything digital — and scan both files with two phones at the size they will actually be used. For the design deep-dive, continue with how to make a QR code with a logo in the middle, and for frames and badges see custom QR codes with logos and frames.

The bottom line: your logo does not need to become the code — it needs to sit safely inside it, recognised at a glance and scanned at the first attempt, on every phone your customers own.

Where logo codes quietly fail in the wild

Three failure scenes repeat. The trade-show banner printed from a screenshot of the code instead of the exported file, soft at three metres wide. The van livery where the code wraps a wheel arch and the pattern bends out of recognition. And the loyalty card where a well-meaning designer shrank the finished code to fit a layout grid, below the size any phone can resolve at arm's length. None of these is a logo problem — all three are production problems, solved by the same habits: always print from the exported SVG or PNG, keep codes on flat surfaces, respect the ten-to-one size rule, and scan a physical proof in the place the code will live before the full run is approved.

Logo into a QR code vs logo inside a QR code: clearing up the confusion

Two different ideas hide behind the same search phrase. Some people imagine the logo's shapes themselves becoming the scannable pattern — a code drawn as the logo. That exists as artwork, but it is fragile: the pattern must follow the QR grid exactly, so heavy reshaping breaks it. What businesses actually use, and what this guide builds, is the second idea: a standard, fully valid QR code with your logo embedded in its centre, carried safely by error correction. When you turn your logo into a QR code with a logo QR code generator, you get both halves of the bargain — instant brand recognition on the outside, boring standards-based reliability underneath.

The distinction matters when a designer offers a fully illustrated code. Illustrated codes can be beautiful for a one-off campaign with heavy testing, but for packaging, signage and cards that must scan for years, the embedded-logo approach is the professional default.

Brand consistency: one logo code system for every touchpoint

The quiet advantage of the generator workflow is repeatability. Once your logo, colours, module shape and error-correction level are fixed, every new destination — menu, store page, review link, Wi-Fi — produces a code that looks like a sibling of the last one. Customers learn the family resemblance faster than any single design, and recognition compounds: the third time someone sees your logo code on a different surface, they scan it with the trust earned by the first two.

  • Save the master settings (colours, shape, logo file, level) in a one-page brand note
  • Generate per-placement codes from the master, never by editing a previous export
  • Keep the destination list with the files, so old print can be traced to its link
  • Re-test the master whenever the logo itself is redesigned

Frequently asked questions

Can any logo be used inside a QR code?

Almost. Simple, high-contrast marks work best. Very detailed or wide logos should be simplified to an icon or stacked version so they stay recognisable at small sizes.

Will a logo QR code work in black and white printing?

Yes. The logo's colours do not matter to the scanner; what matters is that the modules stay dark against a light background and the logo keeps to about 20–25% of the code.

What file format should my logo be?

A PNG with a clean background (or SVG where supported), at least 500 pixels wide, gives the sharpest result inside the finished code.

Do I need a square version of my logo?

It helps. The centre space in a QR code is roughly square, so a compact square icon or badge version fills it cleanly. Wide logos can be used small, but they lose legibility.

QR Code Safety: How to Spot a Fake QR Code Before You Scan It

M Written by Shahrukh Ahmad — November 10, 2026

QR codes are wonderfully convenient — which is exactly why criminals paste their own over legitimate ones. The scam is called quishing, and it is easy to defeat once you know the three checks. This guide is for everyone who scans, and for every business whose codes customers trust.

Key takeaways
  • Quishing is a sticker-and-imitation scam, not a code hack — the defence is reading the destination.
  • Three checks: sticker test, domain test, context test — before you tap, every time.
  • Never enter card or login details via a public code unless the official domain is unmistakable.
  • Businesses: embed codes in the artefact, inspect them, and publish what the real link looks like.

How quishing works — and where it strikes

The attack is physical, not technical: a scammer prints a sticker carrying their code and places it over a real one — on parking meters, charging points, restaurant tables, vending machines and posters. Your scan opens their payment or login page, which imitates the real one closely enough to harvest card details or credentials. A second variant arrives by email or post: a 'delivery fee' or 'bank verification' letter whose code leads to the same fake pages. The code itself is not hacked; it is simply pointing somewhere evil, and a code cannot tell you where it points until software shows you.

That last fact is the defence. Every protection in this guide comes from one habit: look at the destination before you proceed. Modern phone cameras already show the link on scanning — the scam only works on people who tap through without reading it.

The three checks before you tap

Check one — the sticker test: run a finger over codes in public places. A code on a raised, peeling or misaligned sticker over another code is a red flag; legitimate codes are printed as part of the sign, menu or meter itself. Check two — the address test: read the previewed link carefully. The real domain sits immediately before the first single slash — 'parkpay-example.com.evil-site.net' belongs to evil-site.net, not to the parking company. Misspellings, strange suffixes and URL shorteners on payment codes all deserve suspicion. Check three — the context test: ask whether the request makes sense. A parking meter that suddenly demands card details plus your banking password is not a parking meter's behaviour.

  • Sticker over a sticker? Do not scan — pay another way
  • Read the domain in the preview before tapping, every time
  • Payment page asking for unusual credentials? Close it
  • When in doubt, type the official address yourself instead

Safer scanning tools and habits

Use a scanner that shows destinations plainly and warns about odd ones. Your phone's built-in camera does show the link — slow down and read it. For codes you meet in documents or images, an online QR scanner lets you decode without opening anything, so the destination can be inspected first. Never enter card details, passwords or one-time codes on a page reached from a public code unless the domain is unmistakably the official one, and keep banking inside the bank's own app, which no code should ever ask you to leave.

Teach the household version of this page: the people most targeted by posted 'delivery' and 'parking' scams are often the least technical. Three checks, explained once at the kitchen table, protect the whole family.

For businesses: protecting your codes and your customers

If your business places codes in public, assume someone may try to cover them. Print codes as part of the artefact — engraved, embedded in the sign or under the laminate — rather than as stickers where you can. Inspect placements on a schedule and photograph them, so a swapped code is spotted in days, not months. Tell customers what your real destination looks like: 'Our menu codes open menu.yourbusiness.com' on the same sign turns every customer into an inspector. And never train customers to scan codes for payments to odd domains: consistency is what makes the fake stand out.

Report tampering when you find it — to the venue, the platform and, for payment scams, the police and your payment provider. Quishing survives on silence; businesses that warn customers loudly make their whole street a harder target.

Frequently asked questions

Can scanning a QR code hack my phone by itself?

Simply scanning is safe: the risk is the page the code opens. Harm happens when you enter details or install something on a fake destination — which is why reading the link preview first defeats almost every scam.

What is quishing?

Quishing is QR-code phishing: a fake code, usually a sticker over a real one or a code in a scam letter or email, that leads to an imitation payment or login page designed to steal details.

How can I check where a QR code leads without opening it?

Your camera's scan preview shows the address — read the domain before tapping. You can also decode a code from an image with an online QR scanner and inspect the destination first.
